# Berth Brokers: The Housing Swap Trade

## The Problem

Housing is the scarcest resource on the ship (see `housing_and_residential_terraces.md`). Allocation is by grade, employment, family size, and length of service, and the waiting lists for better grades are long. Residents' needs change over time: a couple has a child, a worker is promoted, an elderly parent moves in. The official system can respond to these changes, but slowly.

This gap gave rise to the **berth broker**, a person who arranges swaps of housing between residents. The trade is not officially recognised, but the Hotel Directorate tolerates it, because it reduces pressure on the official queue.

## How a Swap Works

A typical swap involves two households who want to exchange cabins. One family is in a Grade 2 single cabin on a lower terrace and wants a Grade 1 shared cabin on a higher one. A second household has the reverse problem. A broker finds the pair, checks that the grades and sizes are compatible, and arranges the exchange.

The steps are:

1. The broker records each household's cabin, grade, and needs.
2. The broker finds a compatible match, which often takes weeks.
3. Both households agree to the swap and sign a private agreement.
4. The broker submits the swap to the Hotel Directorate, which must approve it.
5. The Directorate moves the households, usually within a week.

The Directorate approves most swaps, provided that the grades balance and neither household is in arrears. The Hotel Directorate can refuse a swap if it would breach the population allocation rules or the fire zone limits (see `ship_structure_and_layout.md`).

## Fees

Brokers charge a fee, typically 20 to 50 ledger credits per household, paid in cash tokens or through the ledger in a separate transaction. Fees are not regulated, but they are capped informally by competition. Some brokers offer a free service in return for a share of the next swap, which is a form of credit that the ship's residents call "favours."

Brokers are required to register with the Hotel Directorate's liaison office, which keeps a register of licensed housing liaisons. Most brokers are registered, but a few operate without licences. The Directorate has warned that unlicensed brokers can be prosecuted for fraud (see `crime_and_justice_in_practice.md`).

## The Trade's Culture

Berth brokers are known for their memories. They keep track of every cabin on several terraces, who lives there, what they need, and when their leases expire. Some keep the information in notebooks, and some on their terminals. The best are said to know more about the residential towers than the Hotel Directorate does.

Brokers have a reputation for discretion. They rarely talk about individual swaps, and they do not reveal their clients' reasons. Many residents use brokers for private reasons, such as moving away from a difficult neighbour, or staying close to a relative who is ill.

## Disputes

The trade attracts disputes. The most common are:

- **Hidden costs.** A household pays a fee and then finds the swap has moved them to a noisier terrace, or to a cabin with a fault.
- **Priority.** A household on the official waiting list complains that a broker's clients jumped the queue.
- **Pressure.** Brokers have been accused of pressing residents to swap when they did not want to move.

The Maritime Court has heard several cases. In one, a broker was found to have taken a deposit from a family for a Grade 1 cabin that did not exist. The broker was fined and banned from the trade for five years.

## The Council's View

The Residents' Council has asked the Hotel Directorate to create an official swap register, so that residents do not need to rely on brokers. The Directorate has piloted an online swap board on the intranet. Early results show that the board works for simple swaps but not for complex ones, which still need a broker's knowledge of the terraces (see `communications_and_intranet.md`).

## Why the Trade Persists

Brokers persist because the official system is slow and the ship is small in one sense: every terrace is known to its residents. A broker's value is in trust and local knowledge. The Hotel Directorate's own staff say, privately, that brokers do a useful job, and that the problem is not the trade itself but the lack of space.
