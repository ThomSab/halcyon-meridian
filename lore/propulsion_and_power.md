# Propulsion and Power Aboard the Halcyon Meridian

## Design Philosophy

The ship is designed to use wind for a large share of its propulsion, with nuclear power providing everything else. Wind power is free but unreliable, so the ship must always be able to proceed under engine power alone. The design target is for sails to supply about **40 percent of propulsion energy** over a typical crossing, with more in favorable winds and less in calms or headwinds.

## Wingsails

The ship carries **36 rigid wingsails**, arranged in two rows of 18 along the centerline. Each is about 110 m tall, with an average chord (width) of about 14 m, giving an area of roughly **1,500 m²** per sail and about **55,000 m²** in total.

A wingsail is an airfoil on a vertical mast. It rotates to set the best angle of attack to the apparent wind, which is the combination of true wind and the ship's own motion. Rigid wingsails are more efficient than fabric sails of the same area because they hold their shape in high winds and produce more lift per unit area.

### Thrust

At a true wind speed of 15 m/s (about 29 knots) on a beam reach, each sail can produce roughly 130 to 180 kN of forward thrust, depending on angle and apparent wind. Across the full set, this is about 5 to 6 MN of thrust, or roughly 40 percent of what the ship needs at design speed.

The ship does not sail close to the wind. Its large hull and high superstructure make it slow to tack, and its turning circle is several kilometres. It is optimized for beam reaches and broad reaches, which account for most of its route across the trade-wind and westerly belts.

### Stowage

In storms, sails are feathered (turned edge-on to the wind) to reduce loading. In very high winds, they are stowed to minimize windage. Sail operation is automated, with a crew of about 1,200 in the Sail Department to monitor and maintain the masts. The Sail Master is responsible for stowage decisions, with authority delegated from the Master.

## Propulsion Requirement

Hull resistance is dominated by frictional drag over the wetted surface. At design speed of 9 knots, the total resistance is roughly 12 MN, including frictional and wave-making components. This requires about **55 MW of effective propulsive power**, or about 75 to 80 MW of input power after propulsive efficiency losses.

## Reactors

The ship's main power comes from **four small modular reactors (SMRs)**, each rated at 160 MWe. The reactors are pressurized water designs, a technology that has been built and operated in many countries. Each sits in a shielded citadel below the waterline in the machinery spaces, with its own containment and cooling loop. Heat is rejected to seawater through a closed-loop heat exchanger.

Refuelling occurs roughly every five to seven years and requires a port with nuclear handling facilities. Between refuellings, the reactors run at high capacity. Reactor operations are supervised by a licensed operating crew, and the Chief Engineer holds the nuclear licence.

Total electrical output is 640 MWe. The average load is about 480 MW, with propulsion drawing up to 120 MW when sails are not contributing.

## Electrical Distribution

Power is distributed at medium voltage through a ring network, with redundant feeds to each terrace, farm, and industrial zone. Six azimuthing pod thrusters, each rated at about 20 MW, provide propulsion and steering. These are electric motors mounted in pods under the hull, similar to those used on large cruise ships.

The ring network is divided into zones. A fault in one zone is isolated automatically, so that the rest of the ship keeps running.

## Backup Power

The ship carries:
- **Diesel generators**, about 40 MW total, which start automatically if reactor output is lost
- A **battery bank** of about 200 MWh, which supplies critical systems such as the bridge, medical facilities, fire suppression, and watertight door controls during a transition
- **Emergency generators** on the top deck, located high above the waterline, to power navigation and communications if the lower decks are flooded

## Energy Use

Approximate allocation of electrical power:

| Use | Share of Load |
|---|---|
| Residential (lighting, heating, cooking, appliances) | ~30% |
| Greenhouses and LED farms | ~20% |
| Water treatment and waste processing | ~12% |
| Industry and manufacturing | ~13% |
| Propulsion (when on engines) | varies, 0 to 120 MW |
| Medical, schools, and public buildings | ~5% |
| Other (transit, cargo handling, pumps) | ~20% |

Energy is the second largest operating expense after wages. The Chief Engineer reports energy use daily to the Board.

## Stabilization

The ship rolls in beam seas, and passengers and cargo are both affected. Two systems reduce roll:
- **Anti-roll tanks**, which pump about 2,000 tonnes of water between port and starboard tanks to counter the roll
- **Active fin stabilizers**, which extend from the hull and adjust their angle to generate counter-moments

These systems reduce roll by about half in typical conditions but are less effective in storms, where roll can still exceed 10 degrees.
