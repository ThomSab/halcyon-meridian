# Batch Clerks: The Message Runners

## The Problem

Bandwidth is scarce (see `communications_and_intranet.md`). The ship's satellite link carries about 8 Gbps for 300,000 people, and residents' messages are rate-limited and sent in batches. Most people send their messages at shift changes, which means the network is busy for a short time and quiet for the rest of the day.

A message does not leave the ship the moment it is written. It waits in a queue until the batch is sent, and the wait can be several hours. Families who are used to instant messaging find this hard, and the batch system created a new profession to manage it: the **batch clerk**.

## What Batch Clerks Do

Batch clerks work in the **shift-change halls**, which are large communal rooms on the Spine next to the rail stations. Each hall has a bank of terminals and a queue desk. Clerks do three things:

- **Collect** messages from residents who cannot send them from their own terminal
- **Sort** messages into priority and non-priority batches, following the traffic rules
- **Deliver** incoming messages that are too large or sensitive to send to the terminal directly, such as printed letters or recorded voice notes

Clerks are part of the Communications Department, with about 40 batch clerks across the ship. Their hall is open from 05:30 to 23:30 ship time, and it closes for one hour during each batch send.

## The Batch Clock

The sending schedule is called the **batch clock**. It runs in four batches each day, timed to the satellite passes and to the lowest-traffic periods of the day. The clock is posted in every shift-change hall and on the intranet.

Clerks tend to know the batch clock better than anyone. They can tell residents the exact time their message will leave the ship, and they can estimate how long it will take to reach the destination. The clerks' estimates are often more accurate than the intranet's, because they know which batches are heavy.

## The Letter Trade

Some residents prefer writing letters to sending messages. Clerks handle this by scanning letters, sending them as data, and printing them at the destination port (where that is possible) or delivering them on paper through the port agent. This is slow, but many residents find letters more personal. Clerks take pride in preserving the handwriting of letters where they can, by scanning them at high resolution.

Letters are one of the few parts of the ship's communications that the Company does not read. The Articles protect correspondence (see `surveillance_privacy_and_data.md`), and clerks swear an oath of confidentiality on joining.

## Culture

Batch clerks have a reputation for calm. They spend their days dealing with people who are anxious about family, and they learn to be patient. Many clerks are themselves former residents who joined after losing contact with family, and they describe the job as a way of staying close to people who are far away.

The shift-change halls have their own customs. Residents often leave small notes for clerks, and some clerks keep a wall of thank-you cards. On the anniversary of the first batch send, the clerks hold a short ceremony in which they read out a message from the first resident who sent one.

## Problems

The job is not without difficulty. The most common problems are:

- **Delays.** A satellite fault can hold a batch for hours or days (see `communications_and_intranet.md`).
- **Upset residents.** Bad news often arrives in the batch, and clerks must deliver it with care.
- **Volume.** At shift changes, the halls can be very crowded. The Communications Department has asked for more clerks at peak times.
- **Temptation.** Some residents ask clerks to send messages outside the traffic rules, or to pass on information about the ship. Clerks are trained to refuse and to report such requests.

## Training

Clerks complete a four-month course covering the traffic rules, data handling, confidentiality, and basic first aid for people in distress. The course includes a module on the ship's emergency procedures, because clerks are often the first point of contact for residents who need help in a crisis (see `emergency_response_and_evacuation.md`).

## The Future

Some in the Communications Department argue that batching is a temporary measure, and that new satellite capacity will remove the need for clerks. Others argue that the batching system gives residents a predictable rhythm, and that clerks provide a human service that a machine would not. The debate is ongoing.
