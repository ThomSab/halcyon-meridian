# Cargo and Freight Contracts Aboard the Halcyon Meridian

## Why Freight Matters

Freight is the largest source of revenue for the ship, at about 55 percent of total income (see `economics_and_trade.md`). The cargo holds and the freight yard are the reason the ship can afford to house 300,000 people at low resident fees. The Company's freight business is run by the Cargo Department and managed commercially by the Chief Operating Officer.

## Capacity

The ship can carry about **12,000 TEU** (twenty-foot equivalent units) of containers, split between the holds and the main cargo deck. The freight yard can hold about 8,000 TEU on deck, and the holds take the rest. Loads are balanced to keep the ship trimmed and to limit hull bending moments (see `ship_structure_and_layout.md`).

Bulk cargo, such as grain or ore, is not normally carried. The holds are fitted for containers, and the Company does not have the bulk handling systems needed for large cargoes.

## Types of Cargo

| Type | Share of Freight | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| General containerised goods | ~55% | Electronics, textiles, machinery, consumer goods |
| Refrigerated containers | ~15% | Food and pharmaceuticals, powered from the ship's supply |
| Project cargo | ~8% | Large or heavy items, such as turbine parts and components for offshore projects |
| Dangerous goods | ~5% | Chemicals and other classes, under strict rules |
| High-value goods | ~7% | Secure storage with extra monitoring |
| Other | ~10% | Empty containers, returns, and specialist cargo |

Refrigerated containers require reliable power. They are plugged into the ship's supply on the cargo deck, and the Engineering Department monitors their temperatures.

## Dangerous Goods

Dangerous goods are carried under the International Maritime Dangerous Goods Code. Each class has its own storage rules, separation requirements, and documentation. The Cargo Department employs certified dangerous-goods officers, and the Master must approve each load.

Some goods are banned outright, including explosives of certain types, unstable chemicals, and some radioactive materials other than those needed for the ship's own reactors. The Company's cargo acceptance policy is reviewed each year by the Board.

## Contracts

Freight is sold under three main types of contract:

1. **Spot contracts**, for single shipments, priced at the current market rate. These are common for smaller cargoes.
2. **Annual contracts**, which commit a shipper to a volume of freight over a year, at a fixed or indexed rate.
3. **Route contracts**, which cover a whole crossing or series of crossings, often with guaranteed space in the holds.

Contracts include clauses on delivery dates, damage, delays, and force majeure. Because the ship is slow and sails on a fixed schedule, many shippers accept longer transit times in return for lower rates.

## Pricing

Freight rates depend on:

- The weight and volume of the cargo
- The route and the ports of call
- The time sensitivity of the goods
- The current demand for space on the Atlantic route
- The fuel and power costs that the Company bears (the ship's power is mostly nuclear, so it is cheaper than fuel oil but more expensive to build)

The Company publishes a rate card for standard cargo, and it sets a separate rate for refrigerated and dangerous goods. Rates are reviewed each quarter. The Company's marketing department works with shipping brokers to secure contracts, and it reports its share of the Atlantic freight market to the Board.

## Cargo Handling

Cargo is handled by the Cargo Department and the freight network (see `internal_transport.md`). The steps are:

1. Arrival of containers by lighter at anchorage, or at berth where available (see `ports_of_call.md`)
2. Inspection and documentation, including customs seals
3. Crane lifting to the cargo deck or into the holds
4. Securing and lashing, using a standard lashing system
5. Stowage plan review by the Chief Mate and the Navigation Officer
6. Monitoring during the voyage, with regular checks of lashings and temperatures

Lashing is checked every day during the voyage. Containers may shift in heavy seas, so the lashing system is designed with a margin for the worst expected roll.

## Stowaways and Theft

Cargo is sealed and monitored, and the Security Detachment checks containers before loading (see `piracy_security_and_threats.md`). Theft from containers is rare, but it does happen, and the Company has agreed with shippers that losses are covered by the cargo insurance (see `insurance_and_risk_finance.md`).

## Cargo and Residents

Cargo competes with residents for space and resources. The holds take up floor area that could be used for housing or farms, and the power needed for refrigerated containers reduces what is available for residents. The Company's planning process balances these needs, and the Residents' Council is consulted before any major change to the freight allocation.

## Customs and Documents

Each load requires commercial invoices, bills of lading, customs declarations, and certificates of origin. The Cargo Department keeps copies of every document for 10 years. Customs authorities at each port may inspect cargo, and the Company's legal team handles disputes.

## Claims and Disputes

Cargo claims are common and are handled by the Claims Office (see `insurance_and_risk_finance.md`). The most frequent claims involve delay, damaged packaging, and temperature failures in refrigerated containers. Serious claims can lead to arbitration under the terms of the contract.
