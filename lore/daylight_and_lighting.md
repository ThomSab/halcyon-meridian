# Daylight and Lighting Aboard the Halcyon Meridian

## The Problem

A ship with 300,000 people spends most of its interior far from windows. Many residential levels are deep inside the hull and superstructure, where daylight does not reach. Lighting is therefore both a comfort issue and a health issue, because light controls the human body clock.

The ship keeps ship time (see `daily_life_and_routines.md`), so its lighting follows a fixed daily schedule, regardless of where the sun is.

## Sources of Daylight

Daylight reaches the ship's interior through four routes:

1. **The Solarium deck.** The top deck has a series of translucent roof panels and open-air parks. Daylight is channelled down through skylights into the Spine. This is the brightest area on the ship.
2. **Spine skylights and upper panels.** The upper edge of the Spine has transparent panels, which admit daylight to the Spine's upper concourse.
3. **Terrace gardens.** Each terrace garden is open to the sky on its roof, so residents on the upper levels of each terrace receive some direct daylight.
4. **Balconies and windows.** Grade 0 suites and some Grade 1 cabins have windows or balconies on the outer edge of the towers.

Cabins without windows receive no direct daylight. Residents who work in the holds, the reactor citadels, or the lower decks may go weeks without seeing the sun.

## Artificial Light

Artificial lighting is provided by LED fixtures throughout the ship. The main design targets are:

| Area | Target Brightness | Colour Temperature |
|---|---|---|
| Residential bedrooms (evening) | 100 to 150 lux | 2,700 K (warm white) |
| Residential living areas | 200 to 300 lux | 3,000 K |
| Spine concourse | 300 to 500 lux | 4,000 K (daytime) |
| Working areas (kitchens, workshops) | 500 lux | 5,000 K |
| Corridors and stairs | 100 lux | 3,500 K |

Lighting is linked to the ship clock. The Spine brightens to full daytime levels at 06:00 and dims to evening levels at 18:00. Residential areas dim at 22:00. Corridors remain at reduced night levels throughout the night.

## Circadian Lighting

Cabins have circadian lighting. Light colour shifts from cool white in the morning to warm amber in the evening, which supports natural sleep timing. Cabins also include a **dawn simulator**: a gradually brightening light that starts 30 minutes before a resident's alarm.

The effect is limited. Studies on the ship show that residents who spend most of their day under artificial light still report later sleep onset and poorer sleep quality than those with daily sunlight. The Medical Office recommends that residents spend at least 30 minutes outdoors each day, which is possible on the Solarium deck.

## Light Pollution

At night, the ship's exterior lights can be seen from long distances. Navigation lights are required by law, and deck lights are kept to the minimum needed for safety. The Navigation Officer reviews external lighting each evening. Lights on the sail masts (see `navigation_and_weather.md`) are the most visible.

Marine life can be affected by bright lights at night, so the Company dims external deck lights at night to reduce disruption (see `environmental_impact_and_ocean_ecology.md`).

## Energy

Lighting uses about 14 percent of the ship's electrical load, or roughly 70 MW on average. This load is included in the residential, greenhouse, and commercial shares listed in `propulsion_and_power.md`. The Lighting Department has replaced most fixtures with LED units and has a programme to add more sensors that dim lights in empty areas.

## Maintenance

Light fittings in the terraces are replaced by the Hotel Directorate. The Solarium panels are cleaned weekly by a team that works from a gantry on the superstructure. Cleaning is suspended in storms.

## Seasonal Effects

The ship's latitude changes over a voyage, so day length changes too. In northern summer on the North Atlantic route, the day is about 15 hours long. In the trade-wind belt, the day is about 12 hours. The ship's clock does not change with these seasons, so light timing relative to ship time shifts slowly across a voyage, which affects residents' sleep.
