# Sail Handling and the Sail Crew Aboard the Halcyon Meridian

## The Sail Department

The Sail Department has about **1,200 crew**, organised under the Sail Master (see `captain_and_command_structure.md`). It is one of the largest departments on board and one of the least visible to residents, because most of its work happens on the upper decks and at the mast bases.

The department is divided into three sections:

- **Mast operations** (about 500 staff): monitoring each wingsail, adjusting trim, and responding to alarms
- **Mast maintenance** (about 450 staff): inspection, composite repair, bearing service, and lubrication
- **Rigging and deck support** (about 250 staff): access platforms, cranes for mast work, and lashing for stowage

## How a Wingsail Is Set

Each of the 36 rigid wingsails (see `propulsion_and_power.md`) is an airfoil on a vertical mast. It can rotate through a full circle, and its flap, or slot, can be adjusted to change camber. The wingsail's angle is set by the sail control system, which takes inputs from:

- Wind sensors on each mast head, measuring speed and direction
- The ship's speed and heading from the Bridge
- Strain gauges in the mast base, which show loads
- The route plan from the Navigation Officer (see `navigation_and_weather.md`)

The control system chooses an angle that maximises forward thrust while keeping loads inside limits. The Sail Master can override it manually at any time, and most adjustments in normal conditions are made automatically.

## Sail Watches

The Sail Control Room, on the Bridge deck, is staffed around the clock. Each watch has four operators, one watch officer, and a rotating mast technician who is on call for the upper decks. Watch patterns follow the ship's usual rotation (see `daily_life_and_routines.md`).

When the ship is under sail, the watch officer confirms the sail plan every hour and records it in the log. Sail performance is shared with the Board each day, because wind-assist savings are part of the Company's freight pricing.

## Stowage and Storms

In heavy weather, the sails are feathered or stowed. Feathering turns the wing edge-on to the wind, which reduces loading. In very high winds, the sails are stowed in a parked position, with the flaps lowered and the masts locked to the deck.

Stowage is one of the most dangerous tasks on the ship. Mast crews work on exposed platforms, and the Sail Department has its own safety officers and a dedicated rescue team. Crew working on the masts wear harnesses at all times, and each platform has a fixed lifeline. No mast work is carried out when the wind exceeds 20 m/s, or when the ship's roll exceeds 8 degrees.

## Icing

On high-latitude routes, icing can build on the masts and sails, adding weight and reducing lift. The ship has de-icing systems that use heated panels on the mast surfaces. The Sail Department monitors icing with sensors and can stow sails early if icing risk is forecast. The main icing risk on the current routes is in the North Atlantic in late winter.

## Efficiency

Sails usually provide about 40 percent of propulsion energy over a typical voyage, with more in favourable winds and much less in headwinds or calms. The Sail Department reports its performance each day. Over the first ten years, the Company's records show a measured sail contribution that matches the design target within a few percentage points.

## Training

Sail crew hold the ship's Sail Operator certificate, which takes 18 months to complete. The course includes aerodynamics, composite materials, mast mechanics, and safe working at height. Trainees start on the lower mast platforms and progress to the upper rigs after 12 months.

## Career Paths

Sail crew can move into navigation, engineering, or marine operations, because the training overlaps with those departments. Many senior officers started in the Sail Department. The Company's sail crew are among the best-paid crew on the ship, reflecting the danger and skill of the work (see `economics_and_trade.md`).
