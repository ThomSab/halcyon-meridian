# Economics and Trade Aboard the Halcyon Meridian

## Ownership

The ship is owned by **Meridian Maritime Holdings**, a private company registered in a small island nation. The company is funded by about 4,000 institutional and private investors. Its charter gives the Board of Directors the power to set strategy, approve budgets, and appoint the Master and Chief Operating Officer.

Residents are not owners. They are contract workers, tenants, and dependents of the Company. Some residents hold shares through a cooperative, but this is limited to a small fraction of the total.

## Revenue

The ship earns money in four main ways:

| Stream | Share of Revenue | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Freight | ~55% | Containers carried on deck and in holds; ~12,000 TEU capacity |
| Resident fees | ~25% | Rent, utilities, and service charges, paid through ledger accounts |
| Manufacturing | ~12% | Precision electronics, pharmaceuticals, and specialty goods made in clean rooms |
| Data and services | ~8% | Ocean and weather data sold to shipping and research clients |

Freight is the largest source of income because the ship's hull is designed to carry cargo. The Company charges freight rates based on weight, volume, and transit time. Because the ship is slow, it competes mainly for bulk cargo and goods that can tolerate longer voyages.

## Costs

The largest costs, roughly in order:

1. **Wages and benefits** for about 38,000 crew and operations staff
2. **Food and water**, purchased in port and produced onboard
3. **Insurance**, which is very high because the ship's value and the number of people aboard are both unprecedented
4. **Port fees and lightering**, since most calls require anchorage and transfers (see below)
5. **Maintenance**, including the cost of in-water inspections and repairs

The ship cannot be drydocked in most existing facilities. Maintenance is performed in-water using divers, underwater robots, and cofferdam systems, with a major refit every seven years in a purpose-built basin.

## Currency: Ledger Credits

Residents are paid in **ledger credits** (LC), an internal unit that exists only on the ship's accounting system. LC cannot be taken off the ship, but can be converted to hard currency at designated port exchange offices under Company rules.

Typical prices, illustrative:

| Item | Price |
|---|---|
| Entry-level monthly wage (unskilled) | ~1,100 LC |
| Skilled monthly wage (engineer, nurse) | ~2,400 LC |
| Monthly rent, shared two-room cabin | ~900 LC |
| Monthly commissary allowance (adult) | ~450 LC |
| Water above the free allowance | 0.4 LC per 100 litres |

Housing consumes a large share of wages, often 35 to 40 percent. This is a direct result of the fixed supply of living space: at roughly 15 m² per person, space is the scarcest resource on the ship. Rents are set by the Hotel Directorate, which adjusts them based on occupancy and the terrace's condition.

## Inflation

The ship has no independent central bank. Ledger credits are backed by the Company's reserves, held in hard currency at shore banks. The Company controls the money supply by controlling how many credits it issues through wages and loans.

Prices of imported goods follow port prices, so inflation on the ship largely reflects world commodity prices. Domestically produced goods (greenhouse produce, fish, water services) can be used to stabilize prices, but only within the limits of the ship's capacity.

## Banking

The **Meridian Credit Union** is a resident-owned cooperative that holds savings and makes small loans. It is separate from the Company but regulated by the Board. Loans are restricted to residents with stable employment. Interest rates are set to be slightly above the Company's cost of capital.

## Labor

Work contracts are typically two years. Contracts specify wage, housing allocation, and the right to return to port. Residents may leave at port calls if they can pay their passage and their remaining obligations.

Strikes are restricted at sea. The Articles of Agreement permit work stoppages only in port, and only after mediation by the Residents' Council. Refusal to perform duties during a voyage can be treated as a serious offense under maritime law.

## Port Calls and Lightering

Only about six ports in the world have the depth (over 45 m) and berth length (over 1.7 km) needed for the ship to dock. Most calls are made at anchor offshore, where the ship's freight and passengers are transferred by **lighters** (smaller ships) and tenders. This is slow and expensive. Each port call takes three to five days, and the ship typically calls at a port every 45 to 90 days.

## Shadow Economy

Some goods trade outside the official system. The most common are spare parts for personal electronics, fresh fruit from port that does not go through the Commissary, and unlicensed alcohol. The Wardens track these markets but enforcement is inconsistent. The Company tolerates small-scale trade in consumer goods to reduce pressure on official channels.

## Taxation

The ship pays no national taxes while at sea. It pays flag registration fees, port fees, and a company levy on freight. Residents pay no income tax to the ship. Instead, they pay rent, utilities, and service fees, which are set at levels intended to cover operating costs and maintain reserves.
