# Research and Marine Science Aboard the Halcyon Meridian

## Why the Ship Is a Research Platform

The ship crosses the Atlantic many times each year, along routes that few research vessels can cover consistently. It also has a large, stable power supply, a permanent staff of engineers and scientists, and sensors throughout its structure. These features make it useful for research, and the Company has built a small research programme around them.

Research is a minor part of the ship's revenue, about 8 percent through its data and services business (see `economics_and_trade.md`). Its main value to the Company is in reputation, in regulatory relationships, and in the data it provides for the ship's own operations.

## The Research Laboratories

The ship has a marine science laboratory on Deck 8, with about 300 square metres of working space. It contains:

- Wet labs with running seawater, drawn from a dedicated intake
- Dry labs for analysis, including microscopes, spectrometers, and a DNA sequencer
- A cold room for samples, kept at 4 degrees Celsius
- A small aquarium for live organism studies
- A computing room with access to the ship's data network

The laboratory is staffed by about 25 scientists and technicians, and it hosts visiting researchers on short contracts. Its work follows the ethical and safety rules of the flag state and the Articles.

## Continuous Ocean Monitoring

The ship carries a suite of instruments that run continuously while at sea:

- **Sea surface temperature and salinity** from sensors on the hull intake
- **Current profiling** using acoustic Doppler instruments mounted under the hull
- **Wave height and direction** from radar and buoy data
- **Plankton and particle sampling** from a towed sensor, deployed when conditions allow
- **Atmospheric data** from sensors on the superstructure, including wind, humidity, and aerosols

Data is streamed to shore through the satellite link, within the limits set by the bandwidth priorities in `communications_and_intranet.md`. Research data is priority category 4, which means it is sent during off-peak times.

## Argo Floats

The ship deploys Argo floats, which are autonomous instruments that drift with ocean currents and measure temperature and salinity to a depth of 2,000 metres. The floats surface every ten days and send their data by satellite. The Company has deployed about 40 floats since the ship entered service, and they are part of an international network that informs weather and climate models.

## Plankton Research

The towed plankton sensor is one of the ship's most useful research tools. It counts and classifies plankton as the ship moves, and it records changes in the ocean's biology along the route. The data is used by marine research institutes to track changes in plankton distribution, which affect fish stocks and carbon uptake in the ocean.

## Marine Mammal Observation

The Lookout Department keeps records of marine mammal sightings along the route, which are shared with regional research networks (see `environmental_impact_and_ocean_ecology.md`). The records help researchers track whale migration and identify areas where ship strikes are most likely. Observers are trained in standard survey methods, and the thermal cameras on the Bridge (see `environmental_impact_and_ocean_ecology.md`) help with night-time observation.

## Visiting Researchers

Visiting researchers may join the ship on guest berths (see `immigration_citizenship_and_visitors.md`). They must have an approved research permit from the Company's Research Committee, and their work must not interfere with the ship's operations or safety. Research projects are reviewed for ethics, data protection (see `surveillance_privacy_and_data.md`), and environmental impact.

Visiting researchers stay for periods ranging from two weeks to a full crossing. They are accommodated in guest cabins on Deck 8, near the laboratory.

## Research on Residents

The Demographic Office and the Medical Office conduct research on the health and wellbeing of residents. Examples include studies of sleep, mental health, and the effects of long voyages on the body (see `daily_life_and_routines.md` and `hospital_and_medical_care.md`). Residents must give informed consent to take part, and the Research Committee reviews all studies involving people. Data is anonymised before publication.

## Reactor and Structural Research

Engineering staff work with university researchers on the long-term behaviour of the hull, the reactors, and the sail masts. Some of this work is published in engineering journals, and some is kept confidential for commercial reasons. The Company's fibre-optic strain sensors (see `ship_structure_and_layout.md`) provide valuable data for structural research, which the Company shares with the classification society and the flag state.

## Ethics

The Research Committee has five members: two Company representatives, two residents elected by the Residents' Council, and an external chair appointed by the Maritime Court. The Committee approves all research aboard, and it can stop a project that raises ethical or safety concerns. Results must be made public unless there is a legal or safety reason not to.

## Publications and the Gazette

Research results are published in peer-reviewed journals, and some are summarised in the Halcyon Gazette (see `arts_media_and_entertainment.md`). The Gazette's science section is written by a staff journalist with a science background, and it is reviewed by the Research Committee for accuracy.

## Schools and Research

Schools aboard use the laboratory for practical science lessons, and the Further Education Centre offers courses in marine biology and oceanography (see `schools_and_education.md`). Children's projects are sometimes included in the data collection programme, with supervision from the laboratory staff.
