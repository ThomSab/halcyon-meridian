# Voyage Dating and Naming Customs

## Dating by Crossings

Residents of the *Halcyon Meridian* often date events by the number of crossings the ship has completed, rather than by calendar year. A resident might say "I was born in the twelfth crossing" or "the storm came in the thirty-first crossing." The Gregorian calendar is used for official records (see `calendar_holidays_and_festivals.md`), but the crossing count is what most people use in everyday speech.

A crossing, in this sense, is one passage from a departure port to a destination port. Lisbon to Bridgetown is one crossing, and the return trip is another. The ship's typical schedule makes about seven crossings a year, so the count is a rough but useful measure of time.

## Why the Custom Arose

The custom came from the ship's rhythm. Residents experience time as a series of voyages, each with its own port, its own weather, and its own departures and arrivals. A year at sea feels like a sequence of chapters, and it was natural to name them by their voyages. The calendar year, by contrast, has no meaning at sea, because the ship keeps its position in the middle of the ocean regardless of season.

The Demographic Office has found that residents who use the crossing count describe their lives in more detail than those who use the calendar. They are more likely to recall the places they have been and the events of each passage.

## Crossing Milestones

Certain crossings are marked by custom:

- **The first crossing** of a resident, marked by a small ceremony on the Spine and a token from the Terrace Committee
- **The tenth crossing**, which is sometimes celebrated by crew and residents as a sign of experience
- **The fiftieth crossing** of the ship itself, which the Company marked with a shared meal in 2073
- **The crossing of a major storm**, named after the event (see `ship_legends_and_folklore.md`)

## Naming Customs

Naming on the ship has developed its own patterns. Parents often choose names that reflect the ship's life, and some names are now common among children born aboard. The most frequent categories are:

- **Port names**, such as Lisbon, Bridgetown, or the names of other ports the ship has visited. Children born during a port call are sometimes given the name of the port.
- **Wind and weather names**, such as Trade, Westerly, or Halcyon itself
- **Ship parts**, such as Spine, Mast, Keel, or Bridge, which are used more often as middle names
- **Crossing names**, given to children born during a particular crossing, often with a number or a famous event

The Chaplaincy and the Registry Office have both recorded these naming trends, and the Registry Office has asked parents to avoid names that might be confused with official ship terms.

## The Birth Name Ceremony

Some families hold a **birth name ceremony** on the Spine, where the child is named in front of the terrace. The ceremony is not legal in itself: the name is recorded by the Master in the registry. But it gives the family a public occasion and a chance to introduce the child to the community. The ceremony often includes a short speech by the terrace representative and a gift from each neighbour, usually a small piece of knitting or a plant from the greenhouse.

## Surnames and Lineage

Surnames are used on the ship as elsewhere. Some families use the surnames of the home country, and some have adopted ship-based surnames. A few residents have taken the surname of their ward or of a well-known crossing, and the practice has been limited by the Registry Office, which requires surnames to be unique within a ward.

## Nicknames

Nicknames are common, especially among crew. They often describe a person's job, their habits, or their origin. A gait coach might be known as "Sway," a lighter hand as "Slack," and a chronist as "Minutes." Nicknames are used informally and are never recorded in official files.

## Naming Disputes

Disputes over names are rare. The Registry Office has refused two names on the ground that they were offensive, and one name was changed after a complaint from the Chaplaincy. Parents may appeal to the Maritime Court, which has upheld the Registry Office's decisions.

## Dating in Official Records

Official records use the Gregorian calendar and the registry's date. Crossing numbers are recorded as a secondary field, because the Demographic Office and the Medical Office both use them to track patterns over time. The crossing count is also printed on birth certificates issued by the Master, which parents often keep as a family record.

## A Resident's View

Many residents describe the crossing count as the way they remember their life. They say that the calendar year feels abstract on the ship, but the crossing count is concrete: it tells them where they were, who they were with, and what the weather was like. For those who have lived aboard for decades, the count is the main record of their life.
