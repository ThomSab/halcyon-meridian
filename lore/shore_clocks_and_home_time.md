# Shore Clocks and Home Time

## The Habit

Many residents keep a second clock in their cabin, set to the time at home or at a family member's location. They call it a **shore clock**. It sits beside the ship's clock, which runs on ship time and is set by the chronists (see `chronists_ship_clock_keepers.md`). Some residents keep two or three shore clocks, one for each of the places that matter to them.

The habit is common, though it is not universal. The Demographic Office estimates that about 40 percent of residents with family ashore keep at least one shore clock. Residents with no close ties to land are less likely to keep one.

## Why People Keep Them

Shore clocks serve several purposes:

- **Staying connected.** A resident can tell whether it is a good time to call home, and can imagine what a family member is doing.
- **Keeping a sense of place.** Ship time is fixed to the departure port and changes by one hour at a time. Many people find that the ship's clock drifts away from their own sense of day and night. The shore clock anchors them.
- **Marking the calendar.** Some residents use the shore clock to keep track of holidays and birthdays in their home country (see `calendar_holidays_and_festivals.md`).
- **Grief and memory.** People who have lost family ashore often keep the clock set to the place where a loved one lives, or where they were buried. Some keep it set to the time at the family home even after the family has moved.

## The Clock Itself

Most shore clocks are simple mechanical or battery clocks, bought in the Harbour Hall or sent through a port agent. Some residents build their own from old electronics, and a small market in the terraces sells parts. The clocks are usually set by hand, and the owner resets them at each port call, when they can check the correct time against the port's official clock.

Some residents use the intranet to check the time at home. The Halcyon Gazette publishes a short time table on its front page, showing the current time at the ship's main ports of call (see `arts_media_and_entertainment.md`).

## Rituals

Shore clocks have acquired small rituals. Common ones include:

- **Calling at the right hour.** Families agree on a fixed time to call or send a message, so that the shore clock tells both sides when it is time to be ready.
- **Setting the clock forward on the anniversary.** Some residents change the shore clock by an hour on the day their home country changes its own clocks, so that it stays correct for their family.
- **Two-clock dinners.** Some households eat one meal at the ship's time and another at the home time, reflecting the time at which their family ashore would eat. The Commissary's meal times make this easy (see `cuisine_and_regional_foods.md`).

## Children and Shore Clocks

Children often learn to read time from their parents' shore clocks. The schools use the shore clocks as a teaching tool, because they show two times at once, which helps children understand time zones (see `schools_and_education.md`). Some children grow up knowing the time in several countries they have never visited.

## The Problem of Drift

Shore clocks can drift as the ship moves, because the difference between ship time and home time changes with each crossing. A resident who keeps a clock set to a fixed home time must adjust the difference every day, and many find this confusing. The chronists' reference table (see `chronists_ship_clock_keepers.md`) makes this easier, and the Ship Time Office prints a copy for residents who ask.

## Disputes

Some residents argue that the Company should adopt home time as the ship's official time for the whole population, so that families are not split by time difference. The Board has rejected this, because the ship's shift system, meal times, and power schedules are all tied to ship time. The Residents' Council has not put the issue to a referendum.

## Shore Clocks and Port Calls

When the ship is in port, many residents check their shore clocks against the local time and set them correctly. The moment is often marked by a small gathering in the terrace common rooms, where residents compare clocks and talk about home. Some residents describe this as the most emotional part of a port call.
