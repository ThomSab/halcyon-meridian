# Childhood and Growing Up Aboard the Halcyon Meridian

## Who Grows Up Here

About **58,000 children** under the age of 18 live aboard the ship (see `population_and_demographics.md`). They are divided into three broad age bands: babies and toddlers aged 0 to 4, school-age children aged 5 to 16, and older teenagers aged 17. Each group has a different routine and different needs.

Children are a visible part of life on the Spine and the terraces. They are also the group most affected by the ship's constraints, especially space, sleep, and the lack of a natural outdoor world.

## Early Years

The youngest children attend nurseries on the residential terraces, one for every two terraces (see `schools_and_education.md`). Nurseries are supervised by the Medical Office, and staff are trained in early childhood care.

Babies are born aboard in the ship's hospital (see `hospital_and_medical_care.md`). The Demographic Office licenses births, and families must meet housing and income criteria (see `population_and_demographics.md`). Children born aboard receive the flag state's nationality, and their births are registered by the Master.

Nursery places are allocated by ballot. Some families use informal childcare from neighbours on the same terrace, which the Hotel Directorate encourages but does not regulate.

## Play Spaces

The ship's play spaces include:

- **Terrace play areas**, one on every second terrace garden, with soft surfaces, climbing frames, and shade
- **The Solarium lawns**, which are the largest open space for children to run, and are closed to adults during school hours on weekdays
- **Indoor play rooms** in the community halls, used during storms and in the evenings
- **The Spine Run**, popular with children on their bicycles and scooters during permitted hours (see `internal_transport.md`)

Play areas are designed with motion in mind. Equipment is fitted with handholds and low railings, because children are more likely to fall when the ship rolls.

## Sleep and Motion

Children are more sensitive to ship motion than adults, and many younger children have sleep disturbance in the first weeks of a voyage. The Medical Office gives families advice on sleep routines, and cabins for families have sway-compensated beds (see `housing_and_residential_terraces.md`).

Seasickness is common among children, particularly those under 8. The Medical Office carries paediatric anti-nausea medicine, and nurses monitor children who are unwell.

## Daylight

Children need natural light for their development and sleep. The Solarium deck is the main source of daylight, and the Company's guidance recommends that children spend at least an hour outdoors each day (see `daylight_and_lighting.md`). Schools and nurseries schedule outdoor time on the Solarium when weather allows.

## Schooling

Schooling is compulsory from age 5 to 16 (see `schools_and_education.md`). Children learn maritime studies from age 9, which includes basic navigation, weather, and ship systems. Many children take part in drills and muster exercises as part of school (see `emergency_response_and_evacuation.md`).

## Teenagers

Older teenagers face particular pressures. Those aged 17 may enter the Further Education Centre, while others start work experience in the ship's departments. Work experience is voluntary below age 16, and the Articles limit the hours and the type of work for those under 18.

Teenagers are the most likely group to use the intranet heavily, and the Company's content rules for young people are stricter than for adults (see `communications_and_intranet.md`). Gaming and social media are limited during school hours, and bandwidth for these services is restricted during the school day.

## Crime and Children

Children are protected under the Articles. The Wardens have a specific protocol for children who are at risk or who commit offences, and the Maritime Court has a separate youth panel. Young offenders are more likely to receive warnings or supervision orders than fines or detention (see `crime_and_justice_in_practice.md`).

Children who are victims of crime receive support from the Medical Office and the Chaplaincy. The Company's safeguarding officer is responsible for children's welfare and works with schools and the Medical Office.

## Port Calls and Children

Children may attend local schools during port calls, and about 5 percent of children do so each year (see `schools_and_education.md`). Families who want their children to see a port may arrange a visit, which the Company's education office supports. Children on port visits must be accompanied by an adult.

## Growing Up Without Land

Children who have spent most of their early years aboard often have a different view of the world from those who grew up on shore. They may find some everyday things, such as trees, animals, and open land, unfamiliar. Schools include visits to the Solarium parks and the greenhouses to give children contact with plants and growing things (see `food_and_agriculture.md`).

Many children describe the ship as home and the sea as ordinary. Some find it difficult to adjust when they leave, and the Company offers support to former residents who need help settling ashore.

## Children's Voices

The Residents' Council has a Youth Forum, which meets monthly and sends a representative to Council sessions. The Forum is elected by children aged 12 to 16, and it has a voice in play space design, school policy, and the Spine's events.
