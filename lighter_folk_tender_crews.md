# Lighter Folk: The Tender Crews

## Who They Are

The ship cannot dock at most ports, so people and freight move between it and the shore by **lighter**, a smaller vessel that runs between the ship's side and the quay or anchorage (see `ports_of_call.md`). The crews who run these boats are called **lighter folk**. They are part of the Port Services section of the Cargo Department, which has about 400 staff, and they run 14 lighters and 10 passenger tenders.

Lighter folk are a distinct group on the ship. They spend more time in port waters and in contact with port officials than any other crew, and they have built up their own habits, language, and superstitions as a result.

## The Boats

Lighters are workboats of 30 to 60 metres. They carry containers on a flat deck and have a cabin, a crane, and a fender system for working alongside the ship's hull, which towers over them. Tenders are smaller, fast passenger boats, which carry up to 150 people each.

The hull is 1,600 metres long and rises about 45 metres above the waterline on the main deck. Lighters work at its side under a fixed pattern, approaching at slow speed, matching the ship's drift, and never coming within the ship's wind shadow in a way that could catch them. Experienced lighter folk describe the approach as "walking into a cliff that is moving."

## Dialect

Lighter folk speak a version of Meridian Common (see `language_culture_and_social_classes.md`) with terms from the port trade. Some words are unique to them:

- **"Hang-off"**: the moment a lighter ties up alongside and takes the weight of its load
- **"Slack"**: a ship-to-lighter line that has gone soft and must be retaken
- **"Shore-face"**: someone who spends too long in port and comes back with port habits
- **"Sea-sick ashore"**: the feeling of unsteadiness that lighter folk get on land after a long day on the water

The dialect is mostly oral, but a few lighter folk keep notebooks of new words, and the Market Authority has asked them to share terms that might appear in contracts.

## Rites

Lighter folk have a set of rites that other crew find strange. Three are common:

- **The first lift.** A new lighter crew member lifts their first container with a coin tucked under the lifting shackle. The coin is returned to them at the end of their first year.
- **The tide note.** Each crew writes a short note about the state of the water before going alongside and leaves it in a tin in the lighter's cabin. The notes are never read by anyone outside the crew.
- **Shore beer.** After the last lighter run of a port call, the crew share a drink on the lighter's deck, toasting the ship and the port. The ship's rules limit alcohol, so the drink is usually a non-alcoholic beer or a cordial, but the ritual is kept.

## Risk

Lightering is dangerous. The main hazards are:
- Swell and wind at the hull, which can push a lighter into the ship's side
- Lines parting under load, which can whip across the deck
- Fatigue on long port calls, when lighters may run 18 hours a day
- Falling between the lighter and the hull during transfers

The Port Services section has the highest injury rate in the Cargo Department. It has introduced fender upgrades, mandatory life jackets, and limits on hours during calls. Lighter folk accept the rules, but many argue that the Company should have built more lighter capacity before it committed to anchorage calls.

## Relations with Port Authorities

Lighter folk are the ship's first contact with many port officials. They carry the immigration, customs, and medical teams out to the ship and bring the Company's staff ashore. Their conduct is part of the Company's reputation (see `immigration_citizenship_and_visitors.md`). The Chief Operating Officer requires lighter crews to take a conduct course before each port season.

## Status

Lighter folk are proud of their work and of the independence that comes with running a boat in open water. Many move from the lighters into the Deck and Navigation Guild, and several of the ship's senior mates started as lighter hands. Others stay in lightering for their whole careers, which the crews regard as the best life on the ship.
