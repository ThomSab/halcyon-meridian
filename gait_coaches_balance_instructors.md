# Gait Coaches: The Balance Instructors

## The Job

A **gait coach** teaches people how to walk, stand, and sleep on a moving ship. The job barely existed before the *Halcyon Meridian*. On shore, balance problems are treated by physiotherapists, but on the ship they are common enough to need their own profession. Gait coaches are part of the Medical Office's rehabilitation section, and there are about 60 of them, mostly trained physiotherapists or former sports coaches.

Their work covers three things: teaching residents how to move with the ship's motion, helping people recover after falls, and advising on the design of spaces to reduce the risk of falls (see `elder_care_and_end_of_life.md`).

## Why the Job Exists

The ship's roll is slow, but its rhythm can be disorienting. Residents describe the feeling as "walking on a tilted floor that keeps tilting back." In storms, the roll and pitch can reach 10 degrees, and the Medical Office's injury records show that falls are the most common cause of minor injury aboard (see `hospital_and_medical_care.md`).

The gait coach's role is to reduce those falls. The Medical Office found that residents who had some training in motion walking had about half as many falls in their first year as those who did not.

## Methods

Gait coaches use a short programme of exercises, usually four sessions over two weeks. The main elements are:

- **Knee-soft walking**: keeping the knees slightly bent to absorb the rhythm of the deck
- **Wide stance**: setting the feet wider to lower the centre of gravity
- **Anticipation**: timing steps to the roll, which residents learn by watching the rail gauges on the Spine
- **Hand discipline**: keeping one hand on a rail or a fixed fitting on corridors, but not holding on so tightly that the arm tires
- **Sleep positioning**: using a bolster or a folded blanket to stop the body rolling in bed

Coaches also teach residents to use the sway-compensated beds (see `housing_and_residential_terraces.md`) correctly, because many people sleep in the wrong position and wake with stiff backs.

## The Roll Map

Gait coaches maintain a **roll map**, a chart of where the ship rolls most on each deck. The map is built from the ship's motion sensors (see `propulsion_and_power.md`) and shows the areas with the worst motion. Coaches use it to advise on which corridors need extra rails, which stairs need tread changes, and which seats are best in the Spine's cafés.

The roll map is also used by the Hotel Directorate and by the Spine's planners. It has led to changes such as the wider stairs on the Harbour Terraces and the reduced number of standing spaces on the rail platforms in the worst conditions.

## Special Roles

Gait coaches have three specialist roles:

- **Storm coaches**, who are on duty during Storm Law and help residents move between shelters (see `governance_and_law.md`)
- **Child coaches**, who teach children balance through games on the Solarium lawns (see `childhood_and_growing_up_aboard.md`)
- **Return coaches**, who help residents who have spent long periods in port readjust to the ship's motion, and who help people returning to land to recover their balance

## Culture

Gait coaches have a distinctive walk, which they say is easy to spot on the Spine. The walk is a loose, flexible stride with the knees soft and the shoulders relaxed. Children sometimes imitate it. Residents who have trained with a coach often say that they walk differently afterwards, and some of them say they walk worse on land, a phenomenon the coaches call "land sway" (see `ship_legends_and_folklore.md`).

Coaches also have a shared joke about the Spine's slope. They say the Spine is perfectly flat, and that the feeling of tilt is entirely the fault of the people walking on it.

## Training

Gait coaches must hold a physiotherapy or sports-science qualification. They then complete a six-month course on motion, balance, falls, and the ship's design. The course includes time on the roll-simulator in the Engineering building on Deck 6, which reproduces storm motion for trainees.

## Evidence

The Medical Office's studies show that residents who have trained with a gait coach report fewer falls, less seasickness in the first two weeks, and better sleep. These results are not universal, and the studies are small, but the programme is one of the most popular services offered by the Medical Office.
