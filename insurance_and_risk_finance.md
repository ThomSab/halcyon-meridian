# Insurance and Risk Finance Aboard the Halcyon Meridian

## Why Insurance Is Hard

No insurer has ever written cover for a vessel carrying 300,000 people, a nuclear power plant, and a freight business worth billions of dollars at once. The Company's insurance programme is one of the most complex in maritime history, and it is a major part of the operating budget (see `economics_and_trade.md`).

Insurance premiums are a significant cost, and they respond quickly to incidents. A single serious accident anywhere in the industry can raise rates for the whole fleet.

## Main Cover

The Company buys several types of insurance, usually through a consortium of insurers and reinsurers:

| Cover | What It Protects | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Hull and machinery | The ship itself, its machinery, and its reactors | Largest single policy. Valued at replacement cost, which is very high |
| Protection and indemnity (P&I) | Third-party liability, including injury to people and damage to other ships or ports | Usually provided through a mutual club, with shared liability across members |
| Cargo | Freight carried on board | Each contract has its own terms, and high-value cargo may have specialist cover |
| Nuclear liability | Liability for any nuclear incident | Governed by international conventions and the flag state's law |
| Loss of hire | Income lost if the ship is out of service | Covers periods of refit, port delays, and damage |
| War and piracy | Attack by hostile groups or states | Expensive in some regions, and the Company avoids those areas where possible |
| Passenger and resident liability | Injury or death to residents, crew, and visitors | Shares cover with the Company's medical and welfare budgets |

## Nuclear Liability

Nuclear liability is handled under international conventions, which set a limit on the operator's liability and require a financial guarantee. The guarantee is provided by a nuclear pool of insurers, and it is one of the largest single components of the Company's premium. The flag state checks the guarantee before the ship can operate.

See `reactor_operations_and_nuclear_safety.md` for the safety measures that reduce the risk.

## Mass Casualty Risk

The ship's insurance must cover the possibility of an incident affecting thousands of people. Underwriters assess this risk using the ship's safety record, the design of its compartments and fire zones (see `ship_structure_and_layout.md`), and its drills (see `emergency_response_and_evacuation.md`).

The most feared scenario is a multi-compartment flood combined with loss of power, which would leave the ship without propulsion in heavy weather. The design and drills are aimed at making this scenario unlikely, but the insurers still price it into the premium.

## Premiums

Annual premiums are paid from the operating budget. Premiums are set by:

- The ship's value and replacement cost
- The safety record and the results of inspections
- The routes, ports of call, and freight contracts
- The state of the insurance market at renewal

The Company's premium is among the highest in the industry, reflecting the scale of the risk. The premium is reviewed each year, and the Chief Operating Officer must justify any increase to the Board.

## Deductibles and Self-Insurance

The Company keeps a self-insurance reserve for losses below the deductible on each policy. The reserve is held in hard currency at a shore bank (see `economics_and_trade.md`), and it is audited each year. The reserve is intended to cover routine claims, such as damage to cabins, minor repairs, and small injuries.

## Claims

Claims are handled by the Company's Claims Office, which is staffed by about 40 people. The office works closely with the insurers and with the Maintenance Department, which provides evidence of damage. Major claims require a surveyor's report, which is often commissioned from a shore firm.

Residents who suffer loss or injury from an incident aboard may claim through the Maritime Court or through the Company's welfare scheme. The welfare scheme pays fixed amounts for injuries and bereavement, so that claims do not need to be litigated in every case.

## Reinsurance and Catastrophe Bonds

Some of the risk is passed to reinsurers, and some is transferred to the capital markets through catastrophe bonds. These bonds pay investors a return if no major incident occurs, and they pay out to the Company if a defined catastrophe happens. The bond structure reduces the Company's exposure to a single event, and it has attracted investors who want exposure to shipping risk.

## Investor Concerns

Investors are concerned about the scale of the ship's risk. The Board reports on insurance and risk each year in the annual report (see `history_and_construction_timeline.md`). The report includes a stress test for scenarios such as a major flood, a reactor incident, and a prolonged loss of freight revenue.

## Risk Management

The Company's risk team reviews the main risks each quarter. The team's top risks are:
- A major flood or collision in a port or at anchorage
- A nuclear safety event
- A prolonged loss of satellite communications
- A major fire in a residential zone
- A piracy or security incident (see `piracy_security_and_threats.md`)
- A fall in freight rates, which would reduce revenue faster than costs

Each risk has an owner, a set of controls, and a target for residual risk. The risk team reports to the Audit Committee of the Board.
