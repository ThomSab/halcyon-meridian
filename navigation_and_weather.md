# Navigation and Weather Aboard the Halcyon Meridian

## Routing Philosophy

The ship's route is chosen to minimize travel time and fuel use, while keeping passengers and cargo as comfortable as possible and avoiding dangerous weather. Speed is a trade-off: the ship travels faster in calm conditions and slows in storms to reduce structural stress and roll.

A typical crossing takes two to four weeks. The Lisbon to Bridgetown route covers about 3,900 nautical miles. At an average speed of 8 knots, this takes roughly 20 days.

## Weather Routing

Routing decisions are made using **ensemble weather forecasts**, which combine many model runs to show the range of likely conditions. These are supplied by commercial weather-routing services over the ship's satellite link. The Navigation Officer and the Master review them each morning in the 05:30 briefing.

The ship typically follows one of three routes, chosen based on forecasts:
- **Southerly route** through the trade-wind belt, where steady northeast winds of 15 to 20 knots favor beam and broad reaches for the sails
- **Gulf Stream route**, which takes advantage of surface currents of 2 to 3 knots flowing northeast, when the destination allows
- **Northerly route**, used only when the southern route is blocked by weather; it has more variable winds and higher wave heights

## Tropical Cyclones

The ship avoids tropical cyclones (hurricanes and typhoons) with a standing rule of keeping at least **300 nautical miles** from any cyclone's forecast track. Cyclone tracks are monitored continuously, and the ship makes route changes several days in advance where possible. Cyclones can move unpredictably, so the margin is conservative.

## Storms and Heavy Weather

When significant wave heights exceed about 8 m, the Master may order the ship to reduce speed and adjust course to take waves at a favorable angle. This is called **heading up** into the swell. The ship may slow to 4 to 5 knots in severe conditions.

During heavy weather, the ship activates **storm stations**:
- Sails are feathered or stowed
- The Sail Department and Engineering crews are on duty at their stations
- Storm Law may be declared (see `governance_and_law.md`)
- Non-essential movement between decks is restricted
- The Medical Office prepares for injuries from falls and motion

In a storm, roll can exceed 10 degrees and pitch can exceed 5 degrees. Passengers and crew who are not in their cabins may be injured, so the Medical Office keeps extra staff on duty.

## Rogue Waves

Rogue waves, which can exceed 25 m in height, are rare but dangerous. The hull is designed to survive them structurally, but damage to bridge windows, deck fittings, and sail machinery is possible. The Navigation Officer monitors reports from ships and satellite sea-state data to avoid areas where rogue waves are more likely.

## Navigation Systems

The ship uses multiple navigation systems in parallel for redundancy:

- **GNSS receivers** for GPS and Galileo positioning, with independent receivers so that one failure does not cause loss of position
- **Inertial navigation** to maintain position if satellite signals are lost, with drift that is corrected when signals return
- **Radar** for detecting other ships, land, and weather systems
- **Automatic Identification System (AIS)**, which broadcasts the ship's position and identity to other vessels and shore stations
- **Electronic chart display and information system (ECDIS)**, which displays the ship's position on digital charts

The Bridge team follows the International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREG). The ship's large size and slow maneuvering make it the "give-way" vessel in almost all encounters.

## Maneuvering

The ship cannot turn quickly. At 9 knots, its turning circle is roughly 6 to 8 km, depending on rudder angle and wind. Stopping from full speed takes roughly 7 to 10 km, several times the length of the ship. Course changes are planned hours ahead, and the Bridge team keeps a constant watch on the surrounding traffic.

Other ships are expected to keep well clear. The Bridge uses radar and AIS to plan safe passing distances, typically at least 5 nautical miles in open water.

## Visibility

The Bridge is about 150 m above the waterline, which gives a radar horizon of about 44 km (about 24 nautical miles). This is useful for detecting ships and land but means that the Bridge must rely on radar, not visual inspection, for distant hazards.

## Night Operations

The ship displays navigation lights on all sides, including masthead and sidelights, stern lights, and deck lighting for crew safety. Lights on the sail masts are visible for many kilometres, which is an important safety feature in busy shipping lanes.

## Port Approach

Port approach is a demanding phase. The ship requires a licensed pilot and at least six tugs for berthing. In most cases, the ship anchors offshore and receives freight and personnel by lighter (see `economics_and_trade.md`). Anchorage must be in water of at least 45 m depth, which limits the locations available.

Anchor handling uses multiple anchors, each weighing several tonnes, set in a pattern to hold the ship against wind and current. Anchor watches are kept continuously.

## Ice

The route avoids known iceberg zones, particularly in the North Atlantic in spring. The ship monitors ice reports from satellite and from other vessels. In the unlikely event of ice contact, the hull is designed to resist minor impacts, and the Bridge follows standard procedures for avoiding ice fields.
