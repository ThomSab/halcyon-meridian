# Knock Codes and Cabin Talk

## The Habit

Residents in the towers often communicate by knocking on walls. The practice began because cabins in the terraces are close together and the partitions between them carry sound. Over time, residents developed a set of simple knock codes that let them signal across the wall without speaking, and the habit became part of everyday life.

Knock codes are used for greetings, check-ins, alarms, and small favours. They are not a formal language, but many residents know a few dozen patterns, and some know hundreds.

## Basic Patterns

The most common patterns are short and easy to remember:

| Pattern | Meaning |
|---|---|
| One knock, pause, one knock | "Are you awake?" |
| Two quick knocks | "Good night" |
| Three knocks, one long | "Come and see this" |
| Five knocks, fast | "I need help" |
| A slow rhythm of four | "The rail is slow tonight" |
| Two knocks, a pause, then one | "Dinner is ready" |

Neighbours often agree private codes, such as a pattern for a child who has fallen asleep or a pattern for a delivery at the door. The meanings are local, and they are not written down.

## The Rhythm of the Walls

The walls in the terraces transmit sound well, and the ship's motion adds its own rhythm. Residents say the knock codes work best in calm weather, when the walls are still. In a storm, the roll can make knocks sound loose, and the patterns become harder to read.

Some residents use the ship's roll as part of the code. A knock timed to the roll is said to carry further than one that is not, and some people claim they can tell the roll from the knock. The claim is not supported by any measurement, but it is part of the lore.

## Use in Emergencies

Knock codes are used in emergencies. The "I need help" pattern is known throughout the towers, and residents are encouraged to respond to it. The Wardens have trained with terrace committees to take the pattern seriously, and in several cases a knock has led to a rescue of a resident who had fallen or fainted. The Emergency Reserve (see `emergency_response_and_evacuation.md`) gives a short course on knock codes for its members.

The codes are not a substitute for the terrace alarm system. Residents are told to use the call button first, and to use the knocks as a second way to reach a neighbour.

## Children and the Walls

Children learn knock codes early, often from older siblings or from the nursery. Parents use them to check on children in the night, and some children use them to talk to friends in the next cabin after lights out. The Hotel Directorate has a rule that the codes must not be used to disturb neighbours after the 22:00 quiet period, but the rule is hard to enforce.

Teachers in the dedicated schools have sometimes used knock codes as a teaching tool for rhythm and counting (see `schools_and_education.md`).

## Cabin Talk

Beyond knocks, cabin talk is a broader habit. Neighbours speak through the walls, leave notes on doors, and share food or small objects by passing them along the terrace corridor. The terrace common room is the usual place for longer conversations.

The thinness of the walls means that privacy is limited, and residents are expected to speak quietly after 22:00 (see `housing_and_residential_terraces.md`). Some residents say that the shared sound makes them feel less alone, while others find it intrusive. The Privacy Committee has said that cabin talk is not a surveillance concern, because it does not involve any system or record (see `surveillance_privacy_and_data.md`).

## Disputes

Knock codes occasionally cause disputes. The most common is when a neighbour knocks at night, and the other resident feels disturbed. Terrace committees usually settle these disputes informally. In more serious cases, the Hotel Directorate may move one of the residents to another cabin.

## Why the Habit Lasts

Knock codes persist because they fit the ship. They work without power, without bandwidth, and without a screen. They also create a sense of closeness between neighbours who might otherwise never speak. Residents who have lived on the ship for years often say that the knock is one of the first things they miss when they go ashore.
