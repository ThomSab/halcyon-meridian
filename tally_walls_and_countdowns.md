# Tally Walls and Countdowns

## The Habit

Many residents keep a **tally wall**: a marked surface in their cabin or on their terrace where they record a count of something that matters to them. The most common counts are:

- **Days to port**, the number of days until the next port call
- **Days to the end of a contract**, for workers on fixed-term contracts (see `economics_and_trade.md`)
- **Crossings completed**, a count of the crossings the resident has made (see `voyage_dating_and_naming_customs.md`)
- **Rolls**, a record of the largest roll felt in a day or a week, in degrees
- **Days since a birthday or a death**, for those marking a personal anniversary

Tally walls are hand-drawn, usually in pencil or chalk, sometimes in paint. They are often decorated, and some are very elaborate. The habit is so common that the Hotel Directorate allows residents to mark their own walls, provided they do not damage the fabric or the fire barrier.

## Port Countdowns

The port countdown is the most widespread tally. Because the ship calls at a port every 45 to 90 days (see `ports_of_call.md`), the countdown is a constant presence in the terraces. Residents mark each day with a stroke, and the strokes often form patterns, such as a bundle of five with a diagonal through it, which is the common notation for a week.

Many residents mark the countdown with a small symbol at the end of each week. The symbols differ by household, and children often invent their own. At landfall, the walls are taken down and photographed, and the photographs are placed in the Landfall Festival's exhibition (see `arts_media_and_entertainment.md`).

## Roll Tallies

The roll tally is an older habit, begun by people who wanted to know how rough the voyage had been. Residents mark the largest roll of each day, and sometimes the number of times the ship rolled more than a set angle. The data is not official, but it is often more memorable than the Company's records.

Roll tallies have a few regular patterns. People in the lower terraces mark bigger rolls than those higher up, although the Company's data says the effect is small. Some residents compare their tallies with the roll map kept by the gait coaches (see `gait_coaches_balance_instructors.md`), and sometimes they find the map and their own memory disagree.

## Contract Countdowns

Workers on fixed-term contracts keep countdowns to the end of their contract. These are often more precise than the port countdown, because the contract's end date is known exactly. Some workers mark each month with a larger symbol, and some write the number of days on the wall in a larger hand as the end approaches.

The contract countdown is a sign of the ship's transient quality. Many residents say that the countdown makes the ship feel temporary, even after years aboard. The Demographic Office has found that residents with a countdown on their wall are more likely to report that they feel unsettled than those without one.

## Mourning Marks

Some residents use the tally wall to mark time after a death. They may record the number of days since a loss, or make a mark on the anniversary. Some draw a small figure, such as a bird or a tree, to mark the person's life. Chaplains say that these marks help people talk about grief, and they are sometimes used in counselling (see `religion_and_chaplaincy.md`).

## Children and Tallies

Children often keep tallies at school or in the nursery, usually of birthdays, port calls, or the number of days until a festival (see `childhood_and_growing_up_aboard.md`). Teachers use the tallies to talk about counting, measurement, and time. Some schools use roll tallies as a lesson in data collection, with children comparing the records of different terraces.

## Care and Removal

The Hotel Directorate asks residents to keep tally marks in pencil or washable chalk, because paint can damage the walls and leave a residue that is hard to clean. When a cabin is reassigned, the walls are cleaned by the Hotel Directorate. Residents are allowed to take photographs of their walls before they leave, and many do.

## Public Tally Walls

Some terraces have shared tally walls, usually in the common room. These record the port countdown for the whole terrace, and they are often used as a community noticeboard. Some terraces paint a shared tally wall with a painted ship or a bird, and residents add their marks around it.

## The Wall as a Record

Taken together, tally walls form a record of life on the ship that the Company's own files do not capture. Historians have begun to photograph them, and the Library's oral history collection (see `ship_legends_and_folklore.md`) includes many accounts of the walls and the people who made them.
